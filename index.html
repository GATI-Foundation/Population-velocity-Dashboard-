Data Notes Indian Global Mobility Dashboard

This dashboard tracks Indians living and working abroad across 10 countries: Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Poland, South Korea, Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States. We measure population, employment, and healthcare for each country, shown two ways: a snapshot (one date) and a flow (over one year).

Quick Reference — Key Definitions
  • Stock vs Flow: Stock = a point-in-time total (e.g., total Indians resident as of year-end). Flow = new arrivals/entries during that calendar year. Different units — never combined on the same chart axis.
  • Indian share of a country's foreign population/workforce = Indian ÷ that same country's own Total Foreigners figure, for the same year. It is never pooled or averaged using other countries' totals in the denominator.
  • "Avg. Indian share" KPIs are a simple, unweighted average of each individual country's own share — not a single global percentage weighted by population size.
  • Each table has its own country list, which differs because the underlying data coverage varies by country and year. Every chart lists exactly which countries are reflected based on the active filters and available data.

1 How the data was sourced

No single international database covers all countries and all indicators. So we combined two types of sources:

🏛️ National official sources
Direct data from each country's government: statistics offices, census operations, central registers, immigration departments. Examples: Statistics Canada, Destatis (Germany), ISA (Japan), ONS (UK), US Census Bureau, INSEE (France), ISTAT (Italy), Statistics Poland, Statistics Korea, INE (Spain).
🌐 International organizations
Multi-country databases: Eurostat (for European residence permits), the OECD (for standardized international migration and healthcare workforce series).

Every number in this dashboard comes from one of these official sources. Nothing is estimated or averaged across sources.

2 What "year" means in this dashboard

The same year label (e.g., "2024") means different things in different countries, depending on how that country records its statistics.

Flow data (new arrivals, permits issued)

Most countries use calendar year (January 1 – December 31). For example: UK long-term international migration, Eurostat first permit issuances, and statistics from Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Poland, South Korea, Spain, and USA.

Exception: US Department of State visa issuance follows the US fiscal year (October 1 – September 30).
Stock data (how many are there now)

3 Important counting differences

Matching sources

When we show "India's share of foreigners" (e.g., "Indians are 2.2% of foreigners"), the Indian number and the total foreigner number always come from the same source and year. We never mix sources in one calculation.

The four primary counting bases

Across the 10 countries, foreign populations are categorized using four distinct methodologies:

1. Citizenship (Passport holders): Germany, Italy, Japan, South Korea, Poland, UK, and France (for visas/permits).
Definition: People holding that country's passport.
2. Country of birth: Canada, France (INSEE Census), USA.
Definition: People born in that country (including those who became citizens).
Note: France uses this for INSEE data but uses citizenship basis for Eurostat permits.
3. Work permits vs. actual employment:
• Permits: Canada, France, Italy, Poland (GUS), South Korea
• Confirmed job: Germany, Spain, Poland (ZUS), USA
Definition: Permit countries count authorization to work. Employment countries count people actually working (from payroll or surveys). These two measures are not the same and should never be compared directly.
4. Country of training (healthcare only): OECD data (All countries except Japan and USA).
Definition: Medical professionals counted by where they earned their degree, not their citizenship.

4 Important notes before reading charts

Before comparing Indian employment across countries: Check which type of count each country uses (Section 3). Different counting methods mean the numbers are not directly comparable. For example, Germany and Spain count actual employment; Canada, France, Italy, and Poland count work permits.
Missing a country from a chart? It likely means that data wasn't available for that year. Full details in the Methodology Note.
